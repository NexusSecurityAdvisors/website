Responsible AI, in practice.

The principles security, professional, and civic communities agree on, and a practical code of conduct your business can adopt this quarter.

Free Guide

Why responsible artificial intelligence is a business issue, not just a philosophy debate.

Customers, employees, insurers, and regulators increasingly ask how a company uses artificial intelligence (AI). Responsible AI is how you answer with confidence: the technology is useful, people are protected, and someone is accountable when it goes wrong.

Trust

People buy from, work for, and share data with organizations they believe will not misuse it. Transparent AI use protects that trust.

Legal exposure

Unfair, deceptive, or discriminatory outcomes are illegal whether a person or an algorithm caused them. "The AI did it" is not a defense.

Quality

Most ethical failures are also quality failures: biased data, untested outputs, and no human checking the result.

Common Ground

The principles nearly every framework agrees on.

Security practitioners, standards bodies, governments, and professional societies use different words, but they converge on the same core ideas.

OWASP

Open Worldwide Application Security Project (OWASP) guidance for responsible, secure AI.

OWASP is a nonprofit, vendor-neutral community whose free, openly licensed guidance is built by volunteers worldwide. Its AI work treats security, privacy, and responsible use as one discipline.

Ethical practices drawn from the OWASP community

  • Openness: share what you learn about AI risks so others can defend themselves too. (OWASP AI Exchange: contribute)
  • Governance first: run AI as a managed program with an owner, inventory, and risk process. (AI program)
  • Least privilege and least model: give AI only the data, permissions, and capability the task needs. (Least model privilege, Data minimization)
  • Assume the model can be manipulated: treat model output as untrusted input and validate it before acting. (LLM01 Prompt Injection, LLM05 Improper Output Handling)
  • Responsible testing: red-team only systems you are authorized to test, and disclose findings responsibly. (GenAI Red Teaming Guide)
  • Privacy by design: minimize, protect, and limit retention of personal data across the AI lifecycle. (OWASP AI Exchange: AI privacy)
  • Human oversight for consequential actions: keep a person in the loop when an agent can send, pay, delete, or decide. (Oversight control, LLM06 Excessive Agency)

In Their Words

What leading organizations say about using AI responsibly.

These are established standards bodies, intergovernmental organizations, professional societies, and research institutes. Their guidance overlaps far more than it differs.

OrganizationTheir core guidance
UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of AI (2021)Adopted by its member states. Puts human rights and dignity first, asks that AI do no harm and be proportionate to its purpose, calls for ethical impact assessments, and says AI should not be used for social scoring or mass surveillance.
OECD AI Principles (2019, updated 2024)Endorsed by dozens of governments. AI should benefit people and the planet, respect human rights and democratic values, be transparent and explainable, be robust, secure, and safe, and have clear accountability.
European Union Ethics Guidelines for Trustworthy AI (2019)Trustworthy AI must be lawful, ethical, and robust, meeting seven requirements: human agency and oversight; technical robustness and safety; privacy and data governance; transparency; diversity, non-discrimination, and fairness; societal and environmental well-being; and accountability.
Council of Europe Framework Convention on AI (2024)The first legally binding international treaty on AI. Requires that AI systems respect human rights, democracy, and the rule of law throughout their lifecycle.
NIST AI Risk Management FrameworkTreats responsible AI as risk management: build a governance culture, map where AI is used, measure risks, and manage them, aiming for AI that is valid, safe, secure, accountable, explainable, privacy-enhanced, and fair.
ACM Principles for Responsible Algorithmic Systems (2022)Computing professionals should ensure legitimacy and competence, minimize harm, protect security and privacy, be transparent and explainable, keep systems maintainable, make decisions contestable and auditable, accept accountability, and limit environmental impact.
IEEE 7000 and Ethically Aligned DesignEngineers should identify stakeholder values early and design them into systems, rather than adding ethics after launch.
Partnership on AIIndustry, academic, and civil-society members recommend responsible practices such as disclosing synthetic media and obtaining consent.
Montreal Declaration for Responsible AITen principles written with public input, including well-being, autonomy, privacy, solidarity, equity, diversity, prudence, and responsibility.
Ada Lovelace InstituteIndependent research institute focused on making data and AI work for people and society, with emphasis on public participation and accountability.
The Alan Turing InstituteThe United Kingdom's national institute for data science and AI; its public-sector guidance stresses fairness, accountability, sustainability, and transparency.

The ethical concerns they raise most often

  • Bias and unfair outcomes for people in hiring, lending, housing, health, and policing
  • Privacy and surveillance from collecting or inferring personal data at scale
  • Deception and misinformation, including deepfakes and undisclosed AI-generated content
  • Lack of accountability when no person owns or can explain an AI decision
  • Safety and security failures, including manipulation of AI systems by attackers
  • Over-reliance that erodes human judgment and skills
  • Impacts on jobs and workers, and the need for training and fair transitions
  • Environmental cost of the energy and water used to train and run large models

Ethical Lenses

Simple ways to test whether an AI use is the right thing to do.

Beyond laws and standards, these questions help leaders decide whether a use of AI fits their values and their customers' expectations.

Philosophical lenses

Ethicists commonly test AI decisions through several lenses: consequences (does it produce more benefit than harm?), duties and rights (does it respect people's rights regardless of outcome?), virtue (would a person of good character deploy it this way?), and care (how does it affect the people with the least power?). A use case that fails any lens deserves a second look.

Shared values

  • Dignity: every person has worth that a system must not override.
  • Honesty: do not deceive people about what is real or who they are talking to.
  • Stewardship: use powerful tools responsibly and for the common good.
  • Justice: protect the vulnerable and avoid unfair burdens.
  • Responsibility: humans remain answerable for what their tools do.

Respecting your people

A responsible AI program makes room for employees with different values. Explain why AI is used, invite concerns through a clear channel, and avoid mandating AI for tasks where a human relationship is the point of the work.

AI Providers

What the major AI companies say about ethical use.

Every major AI provider publishes rules for how its tools may and may not be used. Your business agrees to these terms when employees use the tools, so your AI policy should be consistent with them. Summaries are ours; read each policy for the full terms, which change over time.

ProviderPublished policyKey commitments and limits (summary)
OpenAI (ChatGPT)Usage policiesProhibits uses that harm people, violate privacy, deceive, or bypass safeguards; asks businesses to keep humans accountable for high-stakes decisions.
Anthropic (Claude)Usage Policy; Responsible Scaling PolicyProhibits harmful, deceptive, and privacy-violating uses, adds requirements for high-risk uses such as legal, health, and financial advice, and commits to safety measures that scale with model capability.
Google (Gemini)AI Principles; Generative AI Prohibited Use PolicyCommits to developing AI responsibly with human oversight and safety testing; prohibits dangerous, deceptive, and sexually explicit misuse.
Microsoft (Copilot)Responsible AI principles and standardSix principles: fairness, reliability and safety, privacy and security, inclusiveness, transparency, and accountability.
Meta (Llama, Muse Spark)Use PolicyProhibits illegal, harmful, deceptive, and privacy-violating uses of Meta's models, and requires disclosing AI risks to end users.

The common thread: every provider prohibits using AI to break the law, deceive people, violate privacy, or cause harm, and expects a person to stay responsible for important decisions. Those same themes appear in the ten-point code of conduct below.
Put It In Practice

A ten-point AI code of conduct for small and mid-sized businesses.

Adopt, adapt, and publish these commitments. Each one is backed by the published standards, laws, and guidance linked beneath it.

  1. We disclose AI use to customers and employees when AI interacts with them or helps make decisions about them. Sources: EU AI Act transparency duties (Article 50); Colorado Senate Bill (SB) 26-189 notice requirements; OECD transparency principle
  2. We keep humans accountable. Every AI system has a named owner. Sources: NIST AI Risk Management Framework (RMF) “Govern” function; ISO/IEC 42001; OWASP AI Exchange: AI program
  3. We keep a human in the loop for hiring, lending, health, safety, legal, and other consequential decisions. Sources: EU guidelines: human agency and oversight; OWASP oversight control; OWASP LLM06 Excessive Agency
  4. We never use AI to deceive, including fake reviews, impersonation, or undisclosed synthetic media. Sources: FTC guidance on AI and deception; Partnership on AI synthetic media practices
  5. We protect data. Confidential and personal data goes only into approved tools, and only what is needed. Sources: OWASP data minimization; OWASP AI privacy; UK ICO AI and data protection
  6. We test before we trust, for accuracy, bias, and security, and again after major updates. Sources: NIST AI RMF “Measure” function; OWASP AI Testing Guide; OWASP GenAI Red Teaming Guide
  7. We respect creators, intellectual property, and license terms in what we feed AI and what we publish. Sources: U.S. Copyright Office: Copyright and AI; ACM Code of Ethics
  8. We secure our AI against prompt injection, data leakage, and over-permissioned agents. Sources: OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications; SANS Critical AI Security Guidelines; MITRE ATLAS
  9. We make AI accessible and offer a human alternative when AI does not work for someone. Sources: UNESCO: fairness and inclusion; W3C Web Content Accessibility Guidelines
  10. We listen and correct. People can report AI concerns, and we fix what we find. Sources: ACM principles: contestability and auditability; NIST AI RMF “Manage” function
Red Lines

Uses a responsible business should rule out.

  • Covert manipulation that exploits people's vulnerabilities, age, or emotional state
  • Impersonating real people, voices, or brands without consent
  • Fully automated rejections for jobs, credit, housing, or insurance with no human review or appeal
  • Emotion recognition or biometric categorization of employees or customers without a clear legal basis
  • Generating fake reviews, testimonials, or evidence
  • Feeding regulated or confidential client data into unapproved AI tools

Several of these are prohibited or restricted under the EU AI Act and U.S. consumer-protection law. This page is general guidance, not legal advice.
