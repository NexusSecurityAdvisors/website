The Basics

AI, machine learning, deep learning, and generative AI.

These terms are nested, not competing. Every generative AI tool is machine learning, and all machine learning is AI, but most AI in your business today is not generative.

Under the Hood

How a generative AI request actually flows.

An LLM does not look facts up. It predicts likely output based on patterns learned in training, plus whatever context you give it at the moment you ask. A well-built business deployment wraps the model in controls on both sides.

Training vs. inference

Training is when a model learns patterns from a very large dataset. It is expensive and done by the model provider. Inference is each time you use the model. You pay for inference through per-seat licenses or per-token usage fees.

Why AI "hallucinates"

Because the model predicts plausible text rather than retrieving verified facts, it can produce confident, fluent, and wrong answers, including invented citations. Grounding answers in your documents, asking for sources, and keeping a human reviewer on anything consequential are the standard mitigations.[2]

Tokens and context windows

Models read and write in tokens, roughly three-quarters of a word each. The context window is how much text the model can consider at once. Bigger windows allow longer documents but cost more per request.

A safe-configuration checklist for any AI tool

  • Use business or enterprise plans with single sign-on (SSO) and multi-factor authentication (MFA), never personal accounts for company work.
  • Confirm in writing that your prompts and files are not used to train the vendor's models, and set data-retention to the shortest period that meets your needs.
  • Clean up file-sharing permissions before turning on copilots that search your documents.
  • Turn on data loss prevention (DLP) and sensitivity labels so regulated data such as patient health information (PHI), card data, controlled unclassified information (CUI), or client secrets, cannot be pasted or retrieved.
  • Grant connectors and agents the minimum permissions they need, and require human approval for sending, paying, deleting, or changing records.
  • Enable audit logging, keep logs for your retention period, and review them on a schedule.
  • Publish a one-page AI acceptable-use policy and train employees on it before rollout.
  • Map controls to a recognized framework such as the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) AI Risk Management Framework (RMF)[1] or International Organization for Standardization and International Electrotechnical Commission (ISO/IEC) 42001[11] so customers and insurers can review them.
Prompt Engineering

Better instructions, better answers.

Prompt engineering is the skill of giving an AI model clear context, a specific task, and a defined output. It is the cheapest way to improve AI results, and it requires no code.

The six parts of a strong prompt

  • Role: who the AI should act as ("You are a healthcare privacy compliance analyst").
  • Context: the background it cannot know (audience, company size, constraints).
  • Task: one clear verb ("summarize," "compare," "draft," "critique").
  • Format: the shape of the answer (table, five bullets, 150 words).
  • Examples: a sample of good output, if you have one.
  • Guardrails: what to avoid, and permission to say "I don't know."

Techniques that work

  • Ask it to think step by step, or to list assumptions before answering.
  • Provide the source documents and tell it to answer only from them.
  • Ask for citations and spot-check every one of them.
  • Break big jobs into a chain of smaller prompts.
  • Ask the model to critique its own draft against your criteria.
  • Save prompts that work as shared templates for your team.
Weak promptWrite something about our security for a customer.
Strong promptYou are a security program manager at a 60-person accounting firm.
Context: a new client asked how we protect their tax documents. Use only the attached policy summary.
Task: draft a reply email in plain English.
Format: under 200 words, three short paragraphs, no jargon.
Guardrails: do not promise certifications we do not hold; if the policy does not cover something, say we will follow up.
Security note: never paste passwords, API keys, client records, or regulated data into AI tools that are not approved for that data. Be aware that text hidden in documents, emails, or web pages can carry instructions that hijack an AI assistant. This is called prompt injection and it is the number one risk on the Open Worldwide Application Security Project (OWASP) Top 10 for LLM Applications.[12]

Privacy practices that keep AI out of trouble

  • Know which AI tools touch personal data, and list them in your records of processing and vendor inventory.
  • Minimize: send the AI only the data the task needs, and strip identifiers where possible.
  • Tell people when they are interacting with AI or when AI helps make a decision about them.
  • Keep a human in the loop for hiring, lending, insurance, health, and other consequential decisions.
  • Update your privacy notice and customer contracts to describe AI use and subprocessors honestly.
The Big Picture

How large organizations are using AI, and what it is really delivering.

$2.67T

Global AI spending in 2026

Gartner forecasts worldwide AI spending of about $2.67 trillion in 2026, up 49.5% from 2025, with infrastructure the largest share.[4]

44%

Scaling AI enterprise-wide

In McKinsey's 2026 survey, 44% of organizations said they are scaling AI across the enterprise, up from 38% a year earlier. Nearly nine in ten use AI regularly.[3]

37%

Seeing enterprise-level profit impact

Only 37% report enterprise-level earnings before interest and taxes (EBIT) impact from AI, essentially flat year over year. Adoption is easier than results.[3]

362

Reported AI incidents in 2025

The Stanford AI Index recorded a record 362 reported AI incidents in 2025, a reminder that governance has not kept pace with deployment.[5]

Where large companies are putting AI to work

  • Customer service: AI-drafted replies, chat triage, and call summaries
  • Software engineering: coding assistants and agents for tests and documentation
  • Marketing and sales: content drafts, personalization, and lead research
  • IT and security operations: alert triage, ticket routing, and threat-intel summaries
  • Knowledge management: searchable answers over policies, contracts, and manuals
  • Finance and operations: invoice processing, forecasting, and anomaly detection

What separates winners from stalled pilots

  • They redesign the workflow, not just add a chatbot to it.
  • They pick a few measurable use cases and track results.
  • They clean up data and permissions first.
  • They assign an accountable owner and governance from day one.
  • They train people. MIT NANDA researchers reported in 2025 that most enterprise generative AI pilots they studied showed no measurable profit return, largely because tools were not integrated into real workflows.[6]

Trust

Seven trust factors to check before you rely on AI.

NIST describes trustworthy AI with seven characteristics. Use them as questions for any AI tool or vendor.[1]

CharacteristicQuestion to ask
Valid and reliableHow accurate is it on our tasks, and how was that measured?
SafeWhat happens when it is wrong, and who catches it?
Secure and resilientHas it been tested against prompt injection, data leakage, and abuse?
Accountable and transparentWho owns it, and can we see what data and model it uses?
Explainable and interpretableCan we explain an output to a customer, auditor, or regulator?
Privacy-enhancedWhat data is retained, where, for how long, and is it used for training?
Fair, with harmful bias managedHas it been checked for unequal outcomes across groups of people?

Vendor trust signals

  • A current System and Organization Controls (SOC) 2 Type II report, and ISO/IEC 42001 or ISO/IEC 27001 certification where available
  • A clear data processing agreement with a no-training commitment and a subprocessor list
  • Published model or system cards that describe limitations and testing
  • Admin controls for SSO, retention, logging, and data residency
  • A responsible-disclosure program and a history of fixing reported issues
Benefits & Cautions

The upside of AI and ML, and where to be careful.

Benefits

  • Time back on drafting, summarizing, and searching for information
  • Faster customer response and around-the-clock first-line support
  • Better forecasting and anomaly detection from machine learning
  • Accessibility gains: transcription, translation, and plain-language rewrites
  • Faster security triage and threat-intelligence analysis
  • Small teams can compete on output with much larger ones

Cautions

  • Hallucinations: fluent, confident, and wrong answers, including fake citations
  • Data leakage through prompts, over-shared files, or unapproved tools (shadow AI)
  • Prompt injection and over-permissioned agents taking unintended actions
  • Bias in training data that leads to unfair outcomes for people
  • Intellectual property and copyright uncertainty in generated content
  • Deepfakes and AI-powered fraud aimed at your staff (see How to Spot AI Fakes)
  • Vendor lock-in, rising usage bills, and skills erosion from over-reliance
People & Resources

Who you need to deploy, update, and maintain AI.

AI is not set-and-forget. Models change, vendors ship new versions, data drifts, and regulations evolve. Large enterprises staff every role below. Small and mid-sized businesses usually combine roles and bring in fractional help.

A maintenance cadence that works

Monthly

  • Review usage, cost, and audit logs
  • Triage user-reported errors
  • Check for new shadow AI tools

Quarterly

  • Re-test key prompts and agents after vendor model updates
  • Update the AI inventory and risk register
  • Review permissions and connectors

Annually

  • Refresh the AI policy and training
  • Re-assess vendors and contracts
  • Red-team high-risk use cases and run an AI incident tabletop
