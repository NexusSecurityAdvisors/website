The EU AI Act, explained in plain English.

Who it applies to, what is banned, what high-risk artificial intelligence (AI) requires, what changed in 2026, and seven practical steps for small and mid-sized businesses.

EU AI Act

The world's first comprehensive AI law, explained for businesses.

The European Union (EU) Artificial Intelligence Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, sets rules for AI based on how much risk it poses to people's health, safety, and fundamental rights.[1] It entered into force on August 1, 2024, and applies in stages through 2030. In 2026, the “Digital Omnibus,” Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, delayed the high-risk rules and simplified some obligations for smaller companies.[2][3]

Scope

Does the EU AI Act apply to a U.S. business?

Possibly, even if you have no office in Europe. Like the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), the Act reaches outside the EU.[1]

Your roleWhat it meansApplies when
ProviderYou develop an AI system or model, or have one developed, and sell or release it under your nameYou place it on the EU market, or its output is used in the EU
DeployerYou use an AI system in your business (most SMBs)You are in the EU, or the AI's output is used in the EU
Importer or distributorYou bring AI products made by others into the EU market or resell themYou make them available in the EU

Exemptions include purely personal use, military and national security uses, and scientific research. General information, not legal advice.

Article 5

Prohibited AI practices.

  • Manipulative or deceptive techniques that materially distort behavior and cause significant harm
  • Exploiting vulnerabilities due to age, disability, or social or economic situation
  • Social scoring that leads to unjustified or unfavorable treatment
  • Predicting criminal behavior based only on profiling or personality traits
  • Building facial recognition databases by untargeted scraping of images from the internet or video surveillance
  • Emotion recognition in workplaces and schools, except for medical or safety reasons
  • Biometric categorization to infer sensitive personal traits protected under EU law
  • Real-time remote biometric identification in public spaces for law enforcement, with narrow exceptions
  • From December 2, 2026: AI that generates non-consensual intimate images or child sexual abuse material[2]
High-Risk AI

High-risk AI: where it applies and what is required.

Annex III high-risk areas

  • Biometrics
  • Critical infrastructure (energy, water, transport, digital infrastructure)
  • Education and vocational training
  • Employment, hiring, and worker management
  • Access to essential services, including credit scoring and life and health insurance pricing
  • Law enforcement, migration, and border control
  • Administration of justice and democratic processes

Provider duties (if you build it)

  • Risk management system and quality management system
  • Data governance for training and test data
  • Technical documentation and automatic logging
  • Transparency and instructions for deployers
  • Human oversight by design
  • Accuracy, robustness, and cybersecurity
  • Conformity assessment, CE marking, and EU database registration
  • Post-market monitoring and serious-incident reporting

Deployer duties (if you use it), Article 26

  • Use the system according to the provider's instructions
  • Assign human oversight to people with the necessary competence, training, and authority
  • Make sure input data you control is relevant and representative
  • Monitor operation, report serious incidents, and keep automatically generated logs for at least six months
  • Inform workers and their representatives before using high-risk AI in the workplace
  • Inform people when high-risk AI is used to make or help make decisions about them
  • Public bodies, and private companies using AI for credit scoring or life and health insurance pricing, must complete a fundamental rights impact assessment (Article 27)

The 2026 Digital Omnibus narrowed some high-risk classifications and introduced simplified documentation and quality-management options for small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) and small mid-caps.[2]

Article 50

Transparency duties that affect almost every business using AI.

  • Chatbots and AI agents: people must be told they are interacting with AI, unless it is obvious.
  • AI-generated content: providers of generative AI must mark audio, image, video, and text output in a machine-readable way so it can be detected as AI-generated.
  • Deepfakes: deployers must disclose that image, audio, or video content has been artificially generated or manipulated.
  • AI-generated text on matters of public interest: must be disclosed unless it has been reviewed by a human who takes editorial responsibility.
  • Emotion recognition and biometric categorization: people exposed to these systems must be informed.
AI literacy (Article 4): providers and deployers must take measures to support adequate AI literacy among staff who use AI. The 2026 amendments clarified this as a duty to take supporting measures rather than to guarantee a specific level of skill.[2] Our Leadership AI Workshop and Learning Hub help meet it.
General-Purpose AI

Rules for general-purpose AI models.

Providers of general-purpose AI models, such as the large language models behind popular chatbots, must keep technical documentation, give information to companies that build on their models, have a copyright compliance policy, and publish a summary of training content. Models with “systemic risk” face extra duties, including evaluations, adversarial testing, incident reporting, and cybersecurity protections. These rules have applied since August 2, 2025, and are supervised by the European AI Office.[5] Most SMBs are users, not providers, of these models.

Article 99

Penalties.

ViolationMaximum fine
Prohibited AI practicesUp to €35 million or 7% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher
Most other obligations, including high-risk and transparency dutiesUp to €15 million or 3% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher
Supplying incorrect or misleading information to authoritiesUp to €7.5 million or 1% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher

For SMEs, including start-ups, each fine is capped at whichever amount is lower; the 2026 amendments extended this to small mid-caps.[4][2]

What To Do Now

Seven practical steps for SMBs.

1. Inventory your AI

List every AI tool, feature, and agent your business uses or sells, including those built into other software.

2. Check your EU exposure

Do you sell to, serve, or produce AI output used by people in the EU?

3. Classify each use

Prohibited, high-risk, limited, or minimal. Pay close attention to hiring, credit, insurance, and education uses.

4. Stop anything prohibited

Especially emotion recognition of employees and manipulative design.

5. Add transparency

Label chatbots, AI-generated content, and deepfakes, and update customer notices.

6. Train your people

Document AI literacy measures for staff who use or oversee AI.

7. Use the extra time wisely

High-risk rules start in December 2027. Build governance now with the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) AI Risk Management Framework and International Organization for Standardization and International Electrotechnical Commission (ISO/IEC) 42001, which map well to the Act.
