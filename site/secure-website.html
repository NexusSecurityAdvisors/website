Set up, harden, and maintain a secure website.

Your website is often the first thing an attacker looks at, and a weak one can be defaced, used to phish your customers, or quietly serve malware. This free checklist walks a small business through the same standards security teams use: build it right, lock it down, and keep it that way.

Step 1

Set up: get the foundations right before launch.

Most website breaches start with an account, not code. Lock down who can change your domain, DNS, and site before you publish anything.

Accounts & domain

  • Turn on multi-factor authentication (MFA), ideally passkeys, for your registrar, DNS, hosting, and content management system (CMS) accounts.
  • Use named accounts with the least privilege needed; no shared logins. Remove access the day someone leaves.
  • Enable registrar lock and WHOIS privacy, and keep the registrar contact email on a mailbox you control.
  • Sign your domain with Domain Name System Security Extensions (DNSSEC).
  • Add Certification Authority Authorization (CAA) records so only your chosen certificate authorities can issue certificates for your domain.

Platform & email

  • Prefer a static site or a hosted platform with automatic updates; every plugin and theme is more attack surface.
  • Serve the whole site over HTTPS with an automatically renewing certificate, and redirect all HTTP to HTTPS.
  • Keep secrets (API keys, form tokens) out of your code repository; store them in your host's secret manager.
  • Protect your domain from email spoofing with Sender Policy Framework (SPF), DomainKeys Identified Mail (DKIM), and Domain-based Message Authentication, Reporting, and Conformance (DMARC). Domains that never send mail should publish SPF -all and DMARC p=reject.
  • Use a staging copy for changes, and make deployments repeatable (for example, from a Git repository).
Step 2

Harden: tell browsers exactly what your site is allowed to do.

Security headers and modern encryption settings cost nothing and block whole classes of attacks, including cross-site scripting (XSS), clickjacking, and downgrade attacks. They map to OWASP Top 10:2025 A02 (Security Misconfiguration) and A04 (Cryptographic Failures).

Security headers

  • Strict-Transport-Security (HSTS): max-age of at least one year with includeSubDomains; add preload and submit to the preload list once every subdomain is HTTPS-only.
  • Content-Security-Policy (CSP): start from default-src 'none' or 'self', avoid 'unsafe-inline' and 'unsafe-eval', and set frame-ancestors 'none', base-uri, and form-action.
  • X-Content-Type-Options: nosniff.
  • Referrer-Policy: strict-origin-when-cross-origin or stricter.
  • Permissions-Policy: turn off camera, microphone, geolocation, and anything else you don't use.
  • Cross-origin isolation: Cross-Origin-Opener-Policy: same-origin, Cross-Origin-Resource-Policy: same-origin, and Cross-Origin-Embedder-Policy: require-corp where your embeds allow it.
  • Don't send Access-Control-Allow-Origin: * unless other sites truly need to read your content, and remove headers that reveal server software versions.

Encryption, forms & scripts

  • Allow only TLS 1.2 and 1.3, with modern ciphers (ECDHE key exchange with AES-GCM or ChaCha20); turn off TLS 1.0/1.1 and weak ciphers.
  • Turn off TLS 0-RTT (early data) unless you know you need it.
  • Load scripts from your own domain where possible. For any third-party script, use Subresource Integrity (SRI) or remove it; every tag is a supply-chain risk (OWASP Top 10:2025 A03).
  • If you set cookies, mark them Secure, HttpOnly, and SameSite.
  • Validate every form on the server, add bot protection, and rate-limit submissions.
  • Put a web application firewall (WAF) and bot protection in front of the site; most content delivery networks include one.
  • Publish /.well-known/security.txt (RFC 9116) with a security contact and an expiry date, plus a vulnerability disclosure policy.
Step 3

Maintain: security drifts unless someone owns it.

A site that scored well at launch slips as plugins age, staff change, and new scripts get added. Put these on a calendar and give them an owner.

Monthly

  • Rescan with the free tools below and compare against your last results.
  • Apply CMS, plugin, theme, and dependency updates; turn on automated update alerts (for example, Dependabot).
  • Review DMARC reports for anyone spoofing your domain.
  • Check certificate transparency logs for certificates you didn't request.

Quarterly & yearly

  • Quarterly: review who has access to your registrar, DNS, hosting, and CMS, and remove anyone who no longer needs it.
  • Quarterly: inventory third-party scripts, embeds, and forms, and remove what you no longer use.
  • Quarterly: test that you can restore the site from backup.
  • Yearly: renew the Expires date in security.txt, revisit your CSP, and confirm the registrar and certificate auto-renew.
  • Have a short plan for a defaced or compromised site: who takes it offline, who restores it, and who tells customers.
Step 4

Verify: scan it, read the results, fix what's real.

Free scanners turn this checklist into evidence. The skill is less in running them than in reading them: knowing which findings are real, which are false positives, and which fixes have side effects. Budget about an hour for a first pass, then 15 minutes a month. Scan only sites you own or have written permission to test; some of these tools send probes that look like an attack in your logs.

Run the scanners in this order

  • internet.nl on your bare domain (example.com, not www): the widest single test. It covers IPv6, DNSSEC, HTTPS and TLS, security headers, security.txt, and route origin validation (RPKI)[1].
  • Qualys SSL Labs: certificate chain, protocol versions, cipher suites, and how real browsers connect[2].
  • MDN HTTP Observatory: a graded review of your headers and Content Security Policy (CSP), with suggested fixes[3].
  • Security Headers: a quick header report card[4]. Run it on the bare domain with redirect-following turned off, so you see the headers on the redirect itself.
  • hstspreload.org: checks preload eligibility and tells you exactly what's blocking it[5].
  • A broad external scanner (for example, Sitelemetry[6]): probes for exposed files such as /.env and /.git, open cross-origin resource sharing (CORS), missing Certification Authority Authorization (CAA) records, and server banners. Treat its output as leads to confirm, not verdicts.
  • Google CSP Evaluator and crt.sh: a second opinion on your CSP, and a list of every certificate ever logged for your domain[7].

Read results like an auditor

  • Open one ticket per finding, with the evidence, the fix, an owner, and a retest date. Close it only after a rescan shows it fixed.
  • Confirm before you change anything. Reproduce the finding with a second tool or a direct DNS or HTTP check. Scanners disagree, and some are simply wrong.
  • Know the common false positives. A "missing CAA record" on www is fine when the bare domain has CAA records: certificate authorities climb the DNS tree to the parent domain until they find a CAA record set[8]. A Server: cloudflare banner is a default on some hosting platforms[9] and reveals nothing an attacker can't already see from your IP addresses.
  • Check the timestamp. A report may predate your last deployment, and some scanners cache results for several minutes.
  • Accepting a risk is a decision, not a shrug. Write down why, who approved it, and when you'll revisit it.

Traps that cost points

  • Redirect responses get graded too. If your bare domain only redirects to www, scanners such as internet.nl still check the security headers on that redirect[1]. A redirect created by your CDN never passes through your site's header settings, so add a minimal set with a CDN response-header rule[10]: default-src 'none' CSP, X-Frame-Options: DENY, and a Referrer-Policy. Be honest about what it buys: headers such as X-Frame-Options add little real protection on a redirect[11], so this is mostly about clean scores and consistency.
  • HSTS preload wants two hops. http://example.com must go to https://example.com first, on the same host, and only then to www[5][12]. A single jump from HTTP straight to https://www fails eligibility.
  • Preload is close to permanent. hstspreload.org itself recommends HSTS but not preloading for everyone, and list changes take months to reach browsers[5]. Submit only when you're committed to HTTPS on every current and future subdomain.
  • includeSubDomains breaks forgotten hosts. Before you turn it on, list every DNS record. Any subdomain without a valid certificate (an old webmail or vendor CNAME is the usual culprit) becomes unreachable in browsers, and HSTS gives visitors no way to click past the error[13].
  • Hosting defaults leak. Some static hosts add Access-Control-Allow-Origin: * to every response by default[9]. Override it (for example, by detaching the header[14]), then check the live headers, not just your config file.
  • TLS 1.3-only is the fastest fix for cipher findings, because TLS 1.3 removed the legacy cipher suites[15]. The trade-off: clients limited to TLS 1.2, including some older systems and corporate inspection proxies, can't connect, and NIST guidance for government systems still requires TLS 1.2 support[16]. Decide deliberately. While you're there, turn off TLS early data (0-RTT), which can be replayed by an attacker[15].
  • Dropping 'unsafe-inline' from style-src blocks every style= attribute and <style> block[17], including any HTML your scripts generate. Move them into stylesheets first, then add an automated check so they don't creep back.
  • Cross-Origin-Embedder-Policy require-corp blocks cross-origin images and frames that don't opt in; credentialless loads them without cookies instead[18]. Use it as a middle ground, and leave it off pages that embed third-party frames, such as CAPTCHA widgets.
  • security.txt should list a Canonical URL for every address it's served from, the bare domain included if scanners reach it through a redirect; readers are told not to trust a file fetched from a URL it doesn't list[19].

Keep it green

  • Snapshot your DNS and CDN security settings, and compare them daily against a reviewed baseline. Treat any unexplained change as a possible unauthorized change.
  • Make a failing check open a ticket or send an alert. A check that fails silently for a week is the same as no check.
  • Compare live response headers with your intended configuration on a schedule, not just at deployment.
  • Rescan after every platform, hosting, or DNS change, and at least monthly. Keep the reports as evidence for customers, insurers, and auditors.
  • When you add a subdomain, give it a valid certificate before it goes live; preloaded HSTS will enforce HTTPS on it from day one.
  • These steps support OWASP Top 10:2025 A02 (Security Misconfiguration) and A04 (Cryptographic Failures)[20], and the NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) 2.0 Platform Security (PR.PS) and Continuous Monitoring (DE.CM) categories[21].

Targets

What good looks like.

Use these scores as your finish line. They are free to check and easy to show a board, an insurer, or a customer who asks.

Scores to aim for

  • MDN HTTP Observatory: A+, with every test passing.
  • internet.nl website test: 100% (IPv6, DNSSEC, HTTPS, security options, and RPKI).
  • Qualys SSL Labs: A+.
  • Security Headers: A+.
  • hstspreload.org: eligible, then submitted.

Free resources

Benchmarks and free scanners.

These are the standards and tools this checklist is built on. Only scan sites you own or have permission to test.

Standards & guidance

  • OWASP Top 10:2025: the most critical web application security risks.
  • OWASP Secure Headers Project: which HTTP response headers to send, and which to remove.
  • OWASP Application Security Verification Standard (ASVS): testable security requirements for web applications.
  • OWASP HTTP Headers Cheat Sheet: recommended values for each security header.
  • RFC 9116 (security.txt): generator and standard for publishing a security contact.

Free scanners

  • MDN HTTP Observatory: grades security headers and CSP, with fixes.
  • internet.nl: tests HTTPS/TLS, DNSSEC, IPv6, security headers, security.txt, and RPKI.
  • Qualys SSL Labs Server Test: deep check of your HTTPS configuration and certificate.
  • Security Headers: quick header report card.
  • HSTS Preload List: checks eligibility and submits your domain to browsers' preload list.
  • Google CSP Evaluator: finds weaknesses in a Content Security Policy.
  • crt.sh: searches certificate transparency logs for certificates issued for your domain.
