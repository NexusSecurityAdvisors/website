Secure the devices and accounts where work and life overlap.

Most small-business staff answer work email on a personal phone, open client files on a home laptop, and share a home Wi-Fi network with kids and smart TVs. These free guides help owners and teams close those gaps without buying anything.

For Businesses

Personal devices at work: a bring-your-own-device (BYOD) kit.

If anyone reads work email on a personal phone, you already have a BYOD program; the question is whether it has rules. This kit gives you a plain-English policy, a minimum security bar, and an honest privacy promise to your staff.

Show detailsHide details

1. Pick a model

Company-owned only

Simplest to secure and to wipe. Costs more, and staff carry two phones.

Personal devices with separation

Staff use their own devices, but work apps and data live in a separate, managed space. The most common choice for small businesses.

Company-owned, personal use allowed

The business owns the device and allows reasonable personal use. Make clear that the device can be fully erased.

2. Set a minimum security bar

Any device that touches company email or data must have:

  • A supported operating system with automatic updates turned on
  • A screen lock (6-digit PIN or longer, or biometrics) and encryption turned on
  • No jailbroken or rooted devices
  • Apps only from official app stores
  • Multi-factor authentication on every work account
  • Work data only in approved work apps or the work profile, never in personal cloud storage
  • Report a lost or stolen device to the business the same day

3. Keep work and personal apart

  • Android: Use an Android work profile, which keeps work apps and data in a separate, managed container.
  • iPhone and iPad: Use Apple's account-driven User Enrollment with a Managed Apple Account, which separates work data from personal data.
  • Laptops: Use separate browser profiles, and access company files through the company's cloud apps rather than downloading them.
  • Messaging: Use the company's chat or email for client communication, not personal text messages or social apps.

4. Make an honest privacy promise to your staff

People accept security rules on their own phones when they know exactly what the business can and cannot see. With a work profile or managed account in place:

The business can see

  • Device model and operating system version
  • Whether a passcode and encryption are turned on
  • Work apps, work email, and work files
  • Whether the device meets the minimum security bar

The business cannot see

  • Personal photos, messages, and email
  • Personal browsing history
  • Personal apps and their data
  • Your location, unless this is separately disclosed and agreed to in writing

5. Plan for departures

  • Remove the work profile or managed account (a selective wipe), not the whole device
  • Revoke access and sign out all work sessions the same day
  • Return or transfer any work files the person created
  • A full erase of a personal device happens only if the employee agreed to it in writing beforehand

6. Decide who pays

Common approaches are a monthly phone stipend, reimbursing a share of the plan, or providing company devices to roles that handle the most sensitive data. Put the choice in writing and apply it consistently, and check state rules on reimbursing employees' business expenses.

Windows & Mac

Laptop security checklist.

A lost or infected laptop is one of the most common ways small-business data leaks. Every item below uses settings already built into Windows and macOS.

Show detailsHide details

Updates and protection

  • Turn on automatic updates for the operating system, browser, and apps; restart when prompted
  • Keep built-in protection on: Microsoft Defender on Windows, XProtect and Gatekeeper on Mac
  • Turn on the built-in firewall (Windows Security, or System Settings > Network > Firewall on Mac)
  • Remove software you no longer use, and replace a laptop that no longer receives security updates

Encryption and sign-in

  • Turn on full-disk encryption: BitLocker or Device Encryption on Windows, FileVault on Mac
  • Store the encryption recovery key somewhere safe, such as your password manager, not on the laptop
  • Use a strong password or passphrase, plus Windows Hello or Touch ID for convenience
  • Use a standard (non-administrator) account day to day; keep a separate admin account for installs
  • Lock the screen automatically after 5 minutes or less, and lock it whenever you step away

Browsing and accounts

  • Use a password manager and turn on multi-factor authentication (MFA) for email, banking, and work apps
  • Keep separate browser profiles for work and personal use
  • Install only a few browser extensions you trust; remove the rest
  • Do not save company files in personal cloud storage or personal email

Backup and recovery

  • Follow the 3-2-1 rule: 3 copies, on 2 different types of storage, 1 offline or off-site
  • Test a restore at least twice a year
  • Turn on Find My Device (Windows) or Find My (Mac) so a lost laptop can be located, locked, or erased
  • Never leave the laptop visible in a car, and use a privacy screen when working in public

iPhone & Android

Phone lockdown guide.

Your phone holds your email, banking, authenticator codes, and often your company's data. Thieves and scammers increasingly target the phone number itself to take over accounts.

Show detailsHide details

Every phone

  • Use a 6-digit or longer passcode plus Face ID or fingerprint; set auto-lock to 1 minute
  • Turn on automatic updates, and replace a phone that no longer receives security updates
  • Install apps only from the official App Store or Google Play; delete apps you no longer use
  • Review app permissions for location, microphone, camera, contacts, and photos; choose 'only while using'
  • Hide message previews on the lock screen so codes and private texts are not visible
  • Restart your phone at least once a week, as the NSA recommends, to clear some types of malware
  • Set AirDrop and Nearby Share to Contacts Only, and turn off Bluetooth when you are not using it

Protect your phone number (SIM-swap fraud)

  • Ask your carrier to add an account PIN or passcode and turn on number lock or port-out protection
  • Set a SIM PIN, so a stolen SIM card cannot be used in another phone
  • Use passkeys or an authenticator app instead of text-message codes for email, banking, and work accounts
  • If your phone suddenly loses service for no reason, call your carrier immediately from another phone

iPhone

  • Turn on Stolen Device Protection (Settings > Face ID & Passcode)
  • Keep Find My iPhone on, and use Safety Check to review who has access to your location and accounts
  • Consider Lockdown Mode if you are a likely target, such as an executive, journalist, or official
  • Consider Advanced Data Protection for end-to-end encrypted iCloud backups

Android

  • Keep Google Play Protect on, and use Find Hub (formerly Find My Device)
  • Turn on Theft Detection Lock and Offline Device Lock in theft protection settings
  • On Android 16 or later, consider Advanced Protection for the strongest built-in security
  • Run the Google Security Checkup on your Google account

Home Office

Home office and home network checklist.

When you work from home, your router protects your company. Most home routers are still running factory settings, and every smart device on the same network is a way in.

Show detailsHide details

Router

  • Change the router's default admin password to a long, unique passphrase
  • Turn on automatic firmware updates, and replace a router that no longer receives updates
  • Use WPA3 encryption (or WPA2-AES if WPA3 is not available) with a strong Wi-Fi passphrase
  • Turn off remote administration, WPS, and Universal Plug and Play (UPnP) unless you need them
  • Consider protective DNS that blocks known malicious sites, such as Quad9 or Cloudflare 1.1.1.2

Separate what you can

  • Put visitors, smart TVs, cameras, speakers, and other smart devices on a guest network
  • Keep work laptops off the network that children's gaming devices use, if your router supports it
  • Change default passwords on printers, cameras, and other connected devices, and keep them updated

Workspace habits

  • Lock your screen whenever you leave your desk, even at home
  • Keep smart speakers away from where you take confidential calls
  • Shred printed client or employee information instead of putting it in household trash
  • Use your phone's hotspot instead of public Wi-Fi for sensitive work when traveling

Privacy

Personal privacy and identity protection kit.

Criminals use exposed personal details to open credit in your name, file fake tax returns, and craft convincing scams against you and your business. These steps are free and take an afternoon.

Show detailsHide details

Lock your identity

  • Freeze your credit at Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion; it is free and you can lift it temporarily when you apply for credit
  • Consider freezing your children's credit as well
  • Get an IRS Identity Protection PIN to stop someone else from filing a tax return in your name
  • Create your own my Social Security account before someone else does
  • Turn on transaction alerts for every bank and credit card account

Shrink your exposure

  • Sign up for free breach alerts at Have I Been Pwned for each email address you use
  • Use Google's Results about you tool to request removal of search results showing your phone number or home address
  • Opt out of the largest people-search sites; California residents can use the state's DROP platform to reach every registered data broker at once
  • Set social media accounts to private, and remove birthdays, pet names, schools, and travel plans from public view
  • Use email aliases (such as Hide My Email, Firefox Relay, or SimpleLogin) for shopping and newsletters

Separate personal and business

  • Use separate email accounts, phone numbers, bank accounts, and cards for personal and business use
  • Use a business phone number (for example, a VoIP number) on websites, invoices, and listings instead of your personal cell
  • Keep business files in business-owned storage, not personal cloud accounts
  • List a business address or mailbox instead of your home address wherever possible

Owners & Executives

Digital protection for owners and executives.

Owners, finance staff, and executives are the people attackers impersonate and target first. A few rules stop the most expensive scams, including business email compromise and AI voice cloning.

Show detailsHide details

Stop payment fraud

  • Never change bank or payment details based on an email, text, or call alone; call back on a number you already have
  • Require two people to approve new payees, bank changes, and large or urgent payments
  • Agree on a code word with family and key staff to verify urgent requests, since voices and video can now be faked with AI
  • Treat urgency, secrecy, and gift-card requests as red flags, especially when they appear to come from you

Harden the accounts that matter most

  • Use phishing-resistant MFA (passkeys or security keys) on email, banking, payroll, and your domain registrar
  • Keep account recovery email addresses and phone numbers current, and store recovery codes offline
  • Use delegated access for assistants instead of sharing your password
  • Use separate administrator accounts for IT changes, not your everyday email login

Reduce what attackers can learn

  • Search for yourself and your company: note exposed emails, org charts, home address, and family details
  • Limit public staff directories with direct emails and reporting lines; use role addresses such as info@
  • Be careful what you post about travel while you are away

Travel

  • Bring only the devices you need; consider a loaner laptop and phone for high-risk travel
  • Use your own charger and a wall outlet rather than public USB charging ports
  • Use your phone's hotspot instead of hotel or airport Wi-Fi for sensitive work
  • Update and fully back up devices before you leave

When Something Goes Wrong

Lost a device or think you've been hacked? Your first hour.

Speed matters more than perfection. Print this card and keep it somewhere you can reach without the device you are worried about.

Lost or stolen phone or laptop

  1. From another device, use Find My (Apple) or Find Hub (Android) or Find My Device (Windows) to locate it, lock it, and mark it lost
  2. Call your carrier to suspend the line and block a SIM swap
  3. Tell your employer or IT contact right away if the device had any work email, apps, or files
  4. Change the passwords for your email, banking, and password manager, and sign out of all sessions
  5. If you cannot recover it, erase it remotely
  6. File a police report if it was stolen; insurers and employers often require one
  7. Watch bank, credit card, and account alerts closely for the next few weeks

Hacked account

  1. From a device you trust, change the password and sign out of all other sessions
  2. Check that multi-factor authentication, recovery email, and recovery phone have not been changed
  3. In email accounts, delete forwarding rules and filters you did not create
  4. Change the same password anywhere else you reused it
  5. Warn your contacts not to act on unusual messages from you
  6. If money was sent or stolen, call your bank immediately, then report it to the FBI at ic3.gov and to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov
  7. If your identity was stolen, get a recovery plan at IdentityTheft.gov
  8. If business systems or client data may be involved, do not wipe anything; preserve evidence and call your incident response contact

Personal and business mixed-use self-assessment

1. Do you have a written rule for using personal phones or laptops for work?
2. Is work data kept in a work profile, managed account, or company cloud apps, separate from personal data?
3. Is multi-factor authentication turned on for every work email and cloud account?
4. Do you use separate email accounts for personal and business use?
5. Are automatic updates turned on for every phone and laptop used for work?
6. Is full-disk encryption turned on for every laptop used for work?
7. Have you added a carrier PIN or number lock to protect against SIM swaps?
8. Can you remove company data from a device without erasing the person's personal data?
9. Is the home router's admin password changed from the default, with firmware updated?
10. Do you verify payment or bank-detail changes by calling back on a known number?
11. Are your credit files frozen at all three bureaus?
12. Would staff know exactly who to call if a device with work data were lost tonight?
